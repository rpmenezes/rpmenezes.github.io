Letting Go

On holding on when you expected to let go — and what it took to understand why.

By Ronaldo Menezes
Written in Bristol, United Kingdom

Letting go is hard for most people. For a long time, I assumed I was an exception. I did not think of myself as someone who became deeply attached, and when relationships ended, personal or professional, I believed I moved on with reasonable ease. A recent experience showed me otherwise. I found myself holding on to something I could not release, and the discovery has bothered me enough to make me stop and ask why. This post is part of that reflection.

Looking back, perhaps I should have seen it coming. I believe in the past. I mean that quite literally: the experiences we live through shape who we become, and the person writing this is the accumulated result of every relationship, conversation, success and disappointment that came before. If that is true, then no experience is wasted, and each one deserves to be taken seriously. Perhaps this is why I invest so much of myself in the experiences I have. I go into them fully, with a quiet confidence that, whatever happens along the way, things will turn out well in the end.

That confidence has a particular shape for me. Being positive, in my understanding of the word, means being direct about how I see things. It means being as clear as I can, saying what I think, and making sure people see me for who I am. I have always believed that honesty offered openly tends to be returned in kind, and that clarity, even when uncomfortable, saves everyone time and pain. I still believe most of that. But recent years have taught me that it needs some amendments.

Some of the lessons arrived late. After my divorce, I began to realise how much of my adult life I had spent in a kind of bubble. My ex-wife was, and is, a wonderful person, and the life we built together was stable and kind enough that I rarely had to confront the less generous sides of human behaviour. That protection was a gift. It also meant that when the marriage ended, I stepped into a world I understood far less well than I had assumed. I had been fortunate for so long that I had mistaken good fortune for understanding.

The years since have been mixed, as years usually are. I have had good personal relationships and bad ones. I have had good professional relationships and bad ones. Some people turned out to be exactly who they appeared to be, and some turned out to be something else entirely. Appearances mislead, and often by design. Some people construct realities that do not exist, carefully maintained versions of themselves and of the relationship that hold together only as long as no one looks too closely. And people are frequently less honest than they seem. I am learning, perhaps later than I should, that we must always try to see beyond what is visible, beyond the surface that people choose to show us, to whatever lies underneath.

Here is what I have come to understand at fifty-six, and it took a recent personal experience to teach it to me. The details matter less than the lesson. What happened was something I genuinely did not understand at the time, and in some ways still do not, and it affected me more deeply than I expected. People lie. People can be dishonest, sometimes deliberately and sometimes without quite admitting it to themselves. I have always believed that people are good in the end, and I think that belief is still broadly true. Where I went wrong was in applying it to every individual in every situation.

As someone who spends his working life with data, I find it helpful to think of goodness as an average. Across a lifetime and across all the people someone encounters, a person may be genuinely good. That average says very little about how they will treat any particular person at any particular moment. Someone can be a good person overall and still behave badly towards you. The distribution has tails, and occasionally we are standing in them.

The same applies to me, of course, and this is the part of the lesson I did not expect. Being on the receiving end of something I could not understand sent me back through my own history. I found myself revisiting past events, decisions I had made, things I had said or left unsaid, and seeing them for the first time from the other side. Actions that felt reasonable to me, even justified, may have landed on someone else exactly as this recent experience landed on me: confusing, painful, and impossible to make sense of. I would like to think my own average is decent, but I have certainly been someone else's bad experience, probably more often than I knew. Accepting that goodness is an average means accepting it for everyone, myself included. There is discomfort in that, and also a certain peace.

So why has letting go become so hard for me? I think it is because I look for logic in places where logic does not exist. When something goes wrong, I want to understand it. I replay conversations, reconstruct motives, and search for the explanation that will make the whole thing make sense. I am a scientist by training and by temperament, and I tend to assume that if I think carefully enough, the pieces will fit.

Sometimes they will not. Arguments only work when both people share the same premises. You cannot reason someone out of a position they never reasoned themselves into, any more than you can use evidence to persuade someone who does not accept evidence as the arbiter. The same is true of people's behaviour. Some choices were never made on logical grounds, and no amount of analysis on my part will reveal a logic that was never there. Recognising this is, I think, where letting go begins. The thoughts that keep me trapped are usually attempts to solve a problem that has no solution, and the kindest thing I can do for myself is to stop trying.

Moving forward, I intend to walk into new experiences more carefully. I will still be direct, still be clear, still want people to see me as I am. But I will give trust more slowly, and I will watch for the gap between what people say and what they do before I invest fully.

This comes at a cost. Walking carefully means I may not enjoy the moment as completely as I once did, and I feel that as a genuine loss. Part of what I valued about my old way of living was its openness, the willingness to jump in without checking the depth first. But there must be a gain on the other side. Perhaps it is fewer wounds. Perhaps it is better judgement about where to invest the time I have left. Perhaps it is simply the ability to let go a little faster, having expected a little less.

I still believe in the past. I still believe every experience shapes us, whether we want it to or not. Since I cannot choose what the shaping will be, I will choose more carefully where I go, and how much of myself I bring with me.
