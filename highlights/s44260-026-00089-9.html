The Parenthood Effect in Urban Mobility

Mariana Macedo  ·  Ronaldo Menezes  ·  Alessio Cardillo

npj Complexity, 3: 23  ·  DOI: 10.1038/s44260-026-00089-9

Read the Paper ↗

Household Groups 4 parent / non-parent and married / non-married arrangements compared
Amenity Categories 9 work, residential, leisure, health, food, transport, religious, education, services (OpenStreetMap)

The Problem

Most models of how people move through cities assume an "average citizen" — a single, homogeneous traveller. But cities are not experienced uniformly. A new parent and a young single professional navigate the same streets with entirely different priorities, constraints, and routines. Treating them as interchangeable produces urban policies that fit no one particularly well.

Two of life's biggest transitions — becoming a parent and getting married — profoundly reshape daily mobility. Parenthood introduces caregiving responsibilities, the pull of schools and childcare, and a strong preference for safe, family-friendly neighbourhoods. Marriage brings shared household decision-making, potential dual incomes, and combined residential preferences. Yet these life-altering events have been largely overlooked in traditional mobility research.

The stakes are concrete. In the United States, raising a child to age 18 costs an average of US$233,610 — a figure that underscores how heavily parenthood weighs on household decisions about where to live, work, and travel. As remote work makes relocation more feasible, understanding which cities actually accommodate different household arrangements has never been more relevant.

"People do not experience urban systems in a similar manner. Some cities better accommodate single individuals, others favour married people, and still others are best suited to parents."

Over 55% of the world's population lived in cities as of 2018, rising to a projected 68% by 2050. In the US, roughly 82% already live in urban areas. Building cities that are efficient and equitable demands moving beyond one-size-fits-all mobility models toward designs that recognise demographic diversity.

The Approach

The study combines individual-level mobility data from the US Census with the geolocation of amenities from OpenStreetMap, then uses entropy-based measures to quantify how evenly different household groups access the city — both in where they live and where they travel.

  • Step 01

    Household classification from census data

    Using the 2019 American Community Survey, individuals in 17 US metropolitan areas are classified along two axes: married / non-married (by reported marital status) and parent / non-parent (by number of children). Each traveller carries their home location, work location, and commuting time. New York, the largest area, spans 23 zones and nearly 19.8 million people.

  • Step 02

    Mapping amenities across the city

    Every zone's amenities are extracted from OpenStreetMap and grouped into nine categories — work, residential, leisure, health, food, transport, religious, education, and services. This makes it possible to measure not just how far people travel, but what kinds of destinations they can reach from home and from work.

  • Step 03

    Diversity, mobility diversity, and travel cost

    Three measures are computed per group. Diversity (H) captures the static spatial spread of amenities and homes. Mobility diversity (M) captures the dynamic variety of amenities reachable through commuting. Average cost (C) is the mean commuting time. Comparing groups yields the differences ΔM and ΔC that reveal who lives closer to what.

  • Step 04

    Five null models for robustness

    To confirm the differences are real and not statistical noise, five null models progressively shuffle household labels, travel times, travel distances, and destinations. Cities where parent and married effects survive all five tests (p < 0.05) show the most robust, genuine demographic signatures in their mobility.

Key Findings

Parenthood and marriage leave measurable, consistent fingerprints on how people move through cities — and different cities accommodate those fingerprints very differently.

Cities have demographic "personalities"

Some metropolitan areas better accommodate single individuals (Houston, Virginia Beach), others favour married people (Atlanta, Baltimore), and others are best suited to parents (Cincinnati, Chicago). This classification becomes increasingly relevant as remote work expands where families can choose to live.

Singles travel less; parents reach more

Across most examined cities, non-parents and unmarried individuals spend less time travelling overall. Parents and married individuals instead tend to live in close proximity to a broad range of essential amenities — trading a wider, more diverse footprint of daily destinations for their mobility budget.

Proximity can still mean longer commutes

Living near a diverse set of amenities does not always mean travelling less. In several metropolitan areas, parents' amenity access is coupled with longer average commute times to work — a tension that points to a need for tailored interventions rather than blanket assumptions.

Singles cluster; families spread out

Non-parents and single people tend to be less widely distributed within metropolitan regions, concentrating in particular districts. Parents and married couples are more evenly dispersed — a difference in where each group chooses to root itself within the city.

Amenity type shapes the divide

Leisure and work amenities are more spatially concentrated, while education and health are more evenly spread. Because parents weight schools and clinics heavily and singles weight work and leisure, the geography of amenity types directly drives the differences between the groups.

The patterns survive rigorous testing

The differences are not artefacts of random variation. In the majority of metropolitan areas, parent and married effects persist across null models that shuffle household labels, travel times, speeds, and spatial layouts — a hallmark of genuine demographic effects on mobility.

Why It Matters

Recognising that parents, cohabitants, and single individuals navigate cities differently gives urban planners a foundation for designs that are not just efficient, but equitable — tailored to the real diversity of the people who live there.

  • Tailored Urban Design Rather than one-size-fits-all planning, cities can identify which demographic groups they currently serve well and which they underserve — then target investments (schools, clinics, transit, family-friendly amenities) toward the arrangements that face the highest mobility costs locally.
  • Relocation & Remote Work As remote work loosens the tie between home and office, families and individuals gain more freedom to choose where to live. A public classification of which cities favour singles, couples, or parents gives people a data-driven lens on where their household arrangement will thrive.
  • Equity in Transportation The finding that some groups pay higher mobility costs — sometimes invisibly, through longer commutes despite living near amenities — supports transportation-justice efforts to ensure access is distributed fairly across household types, not just optimised for a mythical average commuter.
  • Sustainable Development Goals The work connects directly to UN SDGs on sustainable cities and inclusive urbanisation. Designing mobility around demographic diversity supports lower emissions, better access to public transport, and more inclusive, resilient urban environments for all residents.
  • Family & Child Well-being Parents' own well-being improves when they spend more time with their children. Understanding how city layouts help or hinder families in reaching schools, healthcare, and safe spaces enables interventions — parks, walkability, road-safety near schools — that measurably improve family life.
