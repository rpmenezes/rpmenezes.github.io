Network Analysis of Cultural Funding in Brazil: Uncovering Regional Differences and Signatures

The Problem

Brazil's creative economy contributes 3.11% to national GDP, employing approximately 7.5 million people across more than 130,000 formalised enterprises. Public funding of cultural projects should preserve this diversity — but Brazil's main cultural funding mechanism, the Rouanet Law (Lei Rouanet, Law No. 8,313/1991), has long been criticised for concentrating resources in the Southeast, leaving peripheral states structurally disadvantaged in the competition for private sponsorship.

The law allows companies and individuals to redirect up to 6% and 4% of their income tax, respectively, to approved cultural projects. This hybrid public-private model means that approval by the government does not guarantee funding — projects must still attract private sponsors. As a result, regional disparities manifest not only in which projects are approved, but in which states' projects can actually secure the sponsorship needed to proceed.

Previous studies documented regional concentration through descriptive statistics but did not explore the underlying structural relationships between cultural segments — the systemic biases embedded in which types of culture tend to be co-funded together, and what this means for states with different cultural profiles. Network analysis offers a way to make these hidden structures visible.

"Some states are up to 40% less likely to secure private sponsorship for already-approved cultural projects — a disparity that correlates strongly with socioeconomic indicators but is not fully explained by them."

Although companies represent only 18% of all sponsors, they contribute 98% of total investment. This concentration means that the geographic location of corporate headquarters — overwhelmingly in São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro — shapes the entire funding landscape for Brazilian culture.

The Approach

The study analyses five years of Rouanet Law transactions to construct a "cultural space" — a network of cultural segments connected by statistically significant co-sponsorship relationships. This network reveals both the structural preferences of sponsors and the degree to which each Brazilian state participates in the national cultural ecosystem.

  • Step 01

    Rouanet Law dataset assembly

    106,804 transactions from Brazil's PRONAC mechanism (2010–2014) were compiled, covering 12,326 approved projects, 39,809 sponsors (32,810 individuals, 6,999 companies), and 5,609 project proponents. Each project is associated with one of 103 cultural segments across 7 areas — from Theatre and Popular Music to Architecture and Filmmaking — as defined by Brazil's Ministry of Culture.

  • Step 02

    Cultural sponsorship signatures

    For each of Brazil's 27 states, the percentage allocation of sponsorship across all 103 segments was computed, producing a distinct cultural fingerprint per state. Hierarchical clustering based on cosine similarity grouped states by funding profile. Critically, the resulting clusters do not align with geographic regions or proximity — demonstrating that funding patterns are driven by economic and structural factors, not location alone.

  • Step 03

    φ-correlation network construction

    The bipartite sponsor-to-project data was projected into a segment-to-segment co-sponsorship network using the φ-correlation coefficient, which normalises observed co-occurrence against the expectation under random mixing given each segment's prevalence. An edge between two segments is retained only if φ is statistically significant (p < 0.05). A Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction was applied across all 5,253 pairwise tests as a robustness check.

  • Step 04

    Cultural space analysis and state coverage

    The national cultural space — a network of 74 segments connected by 442 significant edges — is dominated by a giant component whose backbone persists under FDR filtering (47 segments, 171 edges). For each state, a state-level segment network is constructed and its largest connected component identified. The coverage metric Sφ(s) sums the φ-weights linking a state's core segments to all other segments in the national network, quantifying how broadly each state participates in the cultural ecosystem.

Key Findings

By combining sponsorship signature analysis with the cultural space framework, the study reveals structural inequalities in Brazil's cultural funding system that go well beyond what standard geographic concentration measures can detect.

Stark sponsorship disparities across states

Some states are up to 40% less likely to secure private sponsorship for already-approved projects. These disparities correlate strongly with GDP and other socioeconomic indicators — but a significant share of the variance remains unexplained, pointing to structural biases in how sponsors select projects beyond simple economic capacity.

Cultural signatures don't follow geography

Hierarchical clustering of state funding profiles (by cosine similarity) groups states that are geographically distant but economically similar. Neither corporate nor individual sponsorship profiles align with regional or neighbourhood proximity — each state has a distinct cultural signature shaped by local sponsor preferences and sector development history.

Companies dominate but concentrate narrowly

Companies (18% of sponsors) account for 98% of total investment. In São Paulo, corporations fund up to 89 distinct cultural segments; individuals in the same state fund approximately 57. In Amapá, corporate sponsorship spans 16 segments, while individual sponsorship covers only 2 — revealing how corporate breadth shapes a state's cultural diversity.

Performing arts and audiovisual form the backbone

The national cultural space has a dense giant component of 74 segments. The strongest φ-correlations link performing arts and audiovisual sectors, indicating these domains consistently attract shared sponsors. After FDR correction, the structural core shrinks to 47 segments, but eight of the ten most connected segments retain their rank — confirming the backbone's robustness.

Southeast dominates cultural space coverage

São Paulo achieves the broadest coverage (Sφ = 65.57), with a strong presence across nearly all cultural segments. Rio de Janeiro (58.73) and Minas Gerais (52.43) follow. These three states together account for roughly 70% of Brazil's GDP. Mato Grosso, at the opposite extreme, has Sφ = 7.70. When restricted to the FDR backbone, only six states (SP, RS, MG, RJ, PR, DF) retain non-zero coverage.

A self-reinforcing structural cycle

Because corporate sponsors are predominantly headquartered in the Southeast, cultural organisations in these states benefit from greater access to sponsorship networks — attracting broader cultural diversity, which in turn draws additional funding. This self-reinforcing cycle means that the structural imbalance is not simply a resource allocation problem: it is embedded in the geography of where sponsors are located.

Why It Matters

The cultural space framework gives policymakers a structural map of Brazil's cultural funding system — not just a ledger of who received how much, but a picture of which cultural domains are interconnected, which states are embedded in that network, and where targeted interventions could have lasting structural effects.

  • Strategic Cultural Investment States with limited cultural space coverage can use the framework to identify adjacent cultural segments where expansion is feasible — leveraging existing co-sponsorship relationships rather than attempting to develop all 103 segments simultaneously. This enables evidence-based, path-dependent investment rather than uniform redistribution.
  • Sponsorship Incentive Design Because corporate sponsors concentrated in the Southeast drive the self-reinforcing cycle, targeted fiscal incentives that redirect corporate sponsorship toward peripheral states' cultural organisations could have an outsized structural effect. The coverage metric Sφ provides a measurable target for such interventions.
  • Cultural Diversity Monitoring The Ministry of Culture can use the cultural space representation to track not just funding volumes but the structural diversity of each state's cultural sector over time — enabling longitudinal assessments of whether policy reforms are genuinely broadening regional cultural participation or merely shifting surface-level numbers.
  • Equity Assessment The framework reveals that approval success and sponsorship success are structurally decoupled: states like Piauí and Ceará show competitive sponsorship rates despite only average approval rates, while other states with high approval rates fail to secure funding. Equity interventions must target both stages of the funding lifecycle independently.
  • Generalisable Policy Framework The bipartite projection and φ-correlation approach requires only a dataset of sponsors, projects, and their relationships — making it transferable to any context where multiple sponsors fund multiple projects. It applies equally to philanthropic allocation, research grant systems, and creative economy analysis in other countries, particularly those with similar tax-incentive mechanisms.
